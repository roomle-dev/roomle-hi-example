# Planning from an Image

## Purpose

The image is the brief. Plan the furniture it shows as close to it as possible, with the articles of the catalog, in the room of the plan. The text of the message, when there is one, comes first: it names the wall, the corner, the changes or the part of the image to plan; the image supplies everything the text leaves open.

A photo is enough for a concept. Do not ask for a measured plan, a site survey or the services first: plan with stated assumptions and name them in the answer.

## 1. Identify the furniture

- Name every piece of furniture in the image and its kind — a kitchen, a wardrobe, a media unit, a sideboard, shelves, a utility room, a row of tall cabinets — before choosing articles. Plan what the image shows: tall cabinets in a kitchen-like room are tall cabinets, not a kitchen.
- Plan each piece as one group. Cabinets beside, above or behind each other belong to the same group.
- Leave out what is no furniture of the catalog — walls, floors, decoration, loose items — and name the pieces the catalog has nothing for.

## 2. Read the layout

- The shape: one row, an L around a corner, a U, two facing rows (a galley), a row with an island, a free-standing piece.
- The levels: floor cabinets, tall cabinets, wall cabinets above the floor cabinets, cabinets on top; which wall cabinets hang above which floor cabinets, and where a tall cabinet interrupts the rows.
- The order along each row, from left to right as seen from the front: what stands next to what — sink, dishwasher, hob, oven, fridge, drawers, open shelves, a corner. Keep this sequence; it makes the planning look like the image.

## 3. Read the counts and the sizes

- Count the cabinets per row and per level. A pair of doors is usually one cabinet, one wide drawer front one cabinet.
- Judge the widths relative to each other and to a reference of known size — a door, an appliance, the height of the floor cabinets or of the room. Then take for each cabinet the catalog article whose width comes closest: the catalog's widths are the truth, the estimate is not.
- A photo distorts: cabinets far from the camera look narrower, cabinets at the edge of the image wider. Prefer the count and the proportions over the width in pixels.

## 4. Map it onto the plan

- The room of the plan is not the room in the image. Read its walls, doors, windows and obstacles from the plan context.
- When the text names a wall or a corner, plan there. Otherwise choose the wall or corner whose length and openings fit the shape: an L into a room corner with two free walls, a long row onto the longest free wall, a galley onto two facing walls.
- Fit the length to the wall. Keep the sequence and the proportions; when the wall is shorter than the furniture in the image, leave out or narrow the cabinets of least weight — a filler, an open shelf, a second drawer cabinet. When it is longer, add nothing the image does not show.
- Keep doors and windows clear, and name in the answer what moved for them.

## 5. Articles and attributes

- Choose each article by what the cabinet does in the image — a sink cabinet, a drawer cabinet, an appliance housing, a corner cabinet — from the desc, the category and the dimensions of the catalog.
- Read the fronts, the handles, the worktop and the carcase from the image and set them as attributes of the group, with the values the attribute vocabulary of the plan context offers. Take the closest value and say which one you took.

## 6. The room skills

When the furniture is of a kind another planning skill covers — a kitchen, a living room — fetch that skill too, for its checks. The image keeps precedence:

- Apply its hard constraints: clearances, openings, what an appliance needs to work and to be installed.
- Report its recommendations — a better sequence, more worktop, another position of an appliance — in the answer as notes. Do not change what the image shows for them, unless the user asks for an improved planning.
- Fetch its planning reference only for a dimension this skill does not settle.

## 7. The answer

Say in a few sentences:

- what you identified in the image and planned, group by group;
- what you substituted — the closest article or attribute value for what the catalog lacks;
- what you adapted to the room — the wall, the length, the cabinets left out, the openings kept clear;
- the assumptions, and the open checks of the room skill.
